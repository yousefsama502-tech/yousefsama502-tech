The Award-Winning Synopsis

Exploring the philosophical boundaries between absolute safety and human freedom, trauma, memory, and genetic control.

In 3042, the air was no longer pure oxygen, but rather a thick soup of smart dust, whispering beneath the feet of passersby as if counting their steps. On the ruins of what was once known as the "Sanatorium of Silence", Elijah stood watching his hand tremble under the blue glow of Luminous… a glow that permeated the polluted clouds. It was the poison humanity had once consumed, believing it to be the potion of immortality, only to discover that it was the genetic chains of their eternal enslavement.

Elijah held a secret within his body, terrified it might be exposed: a shake in his fingers unknown to his generation, and a strange emptiness whenever he watched people smile without cause. They all existed in a state of absolute satisfaction, while he felt an undeniable absence… a nameless void. He wasn't hunting for treasure; instead, he was chasing a phantom whispered of by the forbidden storytellers: a man known as the "First Madman."

He ran his fingers over a ruined wall, and a strange coldness invaded his skin from the cracks of the stone. There, beneath the layers of rust and years, he found an inscription not made with a chisel, but with a soul's scream:

"I am Adam… who swore to break the laws of time because they showed no mercy to our childhood. I am the one who dreamed of a glass house to protect the children from the cold world, but the dictators came and stole the glass to make clear coffins for your souls."

The blood froze in Elijah's veins. How could a forgotten child in an orphanage, a thousand years ago, predict the "Genetic Enslavement"? To solve the mystery of his collapsing world, he had to close his eyes and travel back through the dust of centuries, to the cold night where it all began… The night of the "sugar crystal" that was more precious than gold itself.

The orphanage was a merciless place. Its walls dripped with dampness, and the supervisors' hearts dripped with cruelty. Adam, a twelve-year-old boy, realized early on that life was unfair. His seven-year-old little sister, Sarah, was the only reason he woke up every morning. One night, he sneaked into the cold kitchen. Not to eat, but to steal a sugar cube for Sarah, who was worn out by a severe fever. She called sugar "crystals of happiness."

Bernard, a huge and merciless supervisor, caught him and growled: "What are you doing, you little thief?" Adam looked at the sugar in his hand, then at Bernard. He didn't beg. Instead, he said in a steady voice, "She's sick. She needs something sweet to make her smile."

Bernard showed no mercy. His heavy cane came down on the boy's back time after time, but Adam never let go of the cube. He returned to the bed, his body throbbing with pain, yet he was smiling. Then he took off his only shirt and wrapped it around her freezing feet, leaving himself shivering against the wind, while telling her about their future home: "I will build you a house of glass. The sun will shine in from everywhere, and I will plant flowers for you that never wither. I will become a scientist, and I will make science serve children and those in need, so no child will ever get sick again."

Those were the last words Sarah ever heard. In the early hours of the morning, while Adam was trying to warm her hands with his breath, she stopped shivering. She died of neglect, from a fever that could have been cured with a simple dose of medicine. Adam's tears turned into fuel for a sacred fire of cold anger. He swore to break the laws of time that allowed this injustice.

In his bitter solitude, his only companion was Aileen… a witness to the tragedy of genetic extermination. She watched her father, a famous physicist, being dragged away in chains because he refused to change his research to serve the "Obedient Flock" project, and her mother turn into a blank creature with a dull smile after being injected with a primitive drug called "Nexus." At the orphanage roof, she whispered: "Adam, whoever owns the code owns the human. My father died because he wanted us to stay flawed and human, rather than perfect and enslaved."

Adam’s anger became science, and Aileen became his compass. Years later, as symbols in biophysics, the mega-corporation "Eterna" announced its ultimate drug, "Luminous." In their isolated lab, Adam and Aileen built "Sosenus": an optical transmitter that encrypted data into photons, sending them through microscopic time-rifts. On a rainy night in 2090, "Sosenus" caught an encrypted scream from the year 2473 from a young man named Kai: "If you can hear us... do not finish it. You are building the tool Eterna will use to control the past and the future. Do not look at Luminous as a cure. It is a chain that learned how to smile."

When Aileen was diagnosed with brain cancer from prolonged radiation exposure during their tests, Eterna offered to heal her in minutes with an upgraded dose of Luminous in exchange for the Sosenus research. Aileen looked Adam in the eye: "If you inject me with Luminous, my body will heal, but the Aileen you love will be gone forever. Do not kill my soul to save my body." Ultimately sacrificing her consciousness to show Adam the truth, Aileen took the dose—and a week later, she was brain-dead long before her heart ever stopped.

Arrested and thrown into the "Sanatorium of Silence," forcibly injected with Luminous over and over, Adam clung to a single memory—the taste of the sugar cube melting in Sarah's mouth—and used a sharp stone to carve his final testament across a thousand years:

"To the living dead... to whoever reads these lines thinking their chain is a bracelet of light... I am Adam... who swore to break the laws of time because they showed no mercy to our childhood.

I love you, and the pain of losing you tears me apart, but I kiss that pain because it is the only proof that I am still Adam. Luminous is no cure, but an amputation of the soul. How can you know joy if you have never tasted the salt of tears?

These letters are not ink; they are fragments of my soul. Take them, cut your hands on them, bleed, feel pain... for the moment you feel the sting of the wound, you will know Luminous has lost, and that you have turned human once again. Break the glass... let the air in... let life in. —Adam."

When Elijah finished reading the message in 3042, a single hot tear rolled down his cold cheek—the antidote history had been holding out for. Grabbing a sharp stone, he cut his own palm and carved his reply right next to Adam's testament: "I hear you, Adam... the light isn't dead, and the pain is coursing through our veins once again... it is time to wake up." The age of glass was over... the age of awakening had begun.

Chapter One • Full English Translation

Chapter 1: The Sanatorium of Silence

I am a lot older now than the kid I am about to tell you about, but some nights never age, no matter how old we get. I will tell you how things went down exactly as I saw them at seven years old and leave it to you to judge.

The night I rolled into Cydus, the sky was unusually clear. I found out later that the mountain winds sweep the dust away a few hours before dawn, leaving the stars looking like tiny holes punched in a black cloth. I caught a glimpse of them through a narrow crack at the top of the truck wall, and at first, I thought they were beautiful. Then it hit me: they weren't watching over us, and they didn't care about us. They were just too far away, and that was all there was to it.

There were twenty of us crammed into a steel truck, huddled close because the only warmth left in the world was our own body heat. Right in front of me sat a boy with a scar over his eyebrow, a torn shirt, and a lip he was biting down on to hold back his tears. I stretched my foot out until it bumped his. He looked at me and didn't say a thing, but he stopped biting his lip. When you are locked in a steel box, that is all you can really do: remind someone they aren't alone.

My sister Sarah was fast asleep on my right shoulder. She was five, and so tiny that I sometimes forgot she was even breathing. Her little fist was clutching the hem of my shirt like it always did, even in her sleep. That had been our game for the last year: she holds onto me, I hold onto her, and neither of us gets lost.

A year earlier, my mom and dad walked out of our house in Arides and never came back. After that, Sarah and I lived among the ruins, crashing in an old concrete pipe. Last winter, when gray snow blanketed the city, I dug a stale crust out of a dumpster behind an abandoned bakery, broke it in half, handed Sarah the bigger piece, and put on a fake, cheerful voice. "I don't really feel like bread today. I'm stuffed." That night, when I thought she was fast asleep, I felt something tiny being slipped into my palm. It was a handful of fine crumbs she had kept hidden in her fist all day long. "I'm not full, Ado," she whispered. "And you aren't either. I know." From that night on, we had an unspoken rule: whatever we get gets split right down the middle, and we never lie to each other.

Sarah opened her eyes just a sliver in the dark of the truck and mumbled in a sleepy voice, "Ado, tell me a story about the star. The one that never goes to sleep."

"There was once a tiny star, the smallest star of them all, who was terrified she would fall out of the sky if she fell asleep. So, she stayed up every single night, getting more and more exhausted, until the moon finally came over and asked her, 'Why don't you get some sleep?' She told him, 'I'm scared of falling.' The moon just reached out his silver hand, grabbed hers, and said, 'I've got you. You will never fall as long as I am holding on.' So, the little star finally went to sleep, and she never fell. And every night since then, the moon holds her hand and walks her across the entire sky until dawn breaks."

After what felt like hours, the truck screeched to a sudden halt in front of Gate 07. Two men were waiting for us: Bernard, leaning on an ebony cane that tapped the tiles like a slow countdown, and Malum, a massive bald man in a spotless white suit. Bernard spoke in a low tone: "Welcome to the Sanatorium of Silence. I won't ask for your names or who your parents are, because all of that died at the doorstep. Here, you get numbers, you eat based on how hard you work, and you learn that silence is cheaper than speaking up."

Malum clamped a tin bracelet onto every kid's wrist. My bracelet read 417, and Sarah's read 418. During the room lottery, a twelve-year-old girl with sharp hazel eyes scrubbing the tiles deliberately knocked over her steaming water bucket to scatter the slips, sorting them with lightning speed so Sarah and I would both be placed together in Room 4 with her and a four-year-old boy named Arthur clutching a patched-up cloth rabbit.

"I am Lilith," she whispered inside the locked room. "If anyone asks for your name, give them your number. Names are only spoken inside this room, and only in a whisper." I pulled the half-dried date I had saved from my pocket, broke it into four tiny pieces, and handed them to Sarah, Arthur, and Lilith with zero strings attached.

The next morning, Rooms 4 and 5 were paired on work detail alongside Noah, Mira, Casper, and Elias. Elias calculated the cruel math of the facility behind his cracked glasses: "Eight kids today... Grand total is twenty-three Coins. And we need twenty-four, because every kid needs three Coins. Whoever isn't hungry won't work harder. Whoever is starving dies. But whoever is just a little bit hungry every single day works harder. That is their math."

To protect Casper's smog-damaged lungs from hitting day three of coughing—which meant being dragged to the basement—I volunteered to climb the pitch-black north wing smokestack with a rope tied around my waist while Noah hummed our mother's lullaby from below. Right at the bend in the smokestack, under a razor-thin sliver of gray light, I saw dozens of tiny names scratched into the soot-covered bricks by fingernails: Jude. Rayan. Salma. Youssef. Haneen. And at the bottom: "We were here."

At sunset, after paying sixteen Coins for soup at the window of the lobotomized kitchen worker ("Mr. Thank You"), only seven Coins remained—seven small loaves of bread for eight starving children. When the older kids began offering to skip their turn, an idea hit me:

"We split every single loaf into eight pieces," I said. "Then every one of us takes seven pieces."
Elias stared at the tray behind his cracked glasses. "Seven loaves for eight people... everyone takes seven-eighths. Nobody starves alone."
"Yeah," I said. "Hunger gets smaller if you split it up. But nobody can carry all of it on their own."

We split the bread using the sharp edge of Elias's thumbnail until there were fifty-six tiny chunks sitting in front of us. It was the smallest meal I had ever eaten in my entire life. And it was the best meal, too.

That night, through the tiny air vent between Rooms 4 and 5, the children whispered the nightly roll call of the nineteen kids who had disappeared so no one could ever convince them they hadn't existed. Before drifting off, Lilith whispered in the dark: “My grandpa said that names are tiny houses… committing them to memory keeps a roof over their head. However, letting them fade into oblivion means leaving them out to rot. I am keeping eighteen houses safe in my head. And today, I had to build one for Leith. If I go down, I need you to promise me… promise me you will build a roof for me.”

Personal Essay • The Real-Life Story Behind the Novel

Keeping a Roof Over Their Heads

“Names are little houses; keeping them in memory keeps a roof over their heads, while leaving them to be forgotten leaves them to rot in the open.” I wrote this opening line in my novel, A Message from the Time Cell, yet lived its weight years earlier. When I was eight, my six-year-old sister passed away. Days before she died, sensing her terror, I told her about a little star afraid of falling asleep and dropping from the sky, until the moon held her hand and promised she would never fall. Still, she died. Worse, within just a few years of her death, she had begun fading from family conversations, as if she had never existed.

I didn’t grasp the cruelty of that fading until a failed farming project left my family stranded for two years in an unfinished, roofless rest house. On rainy nights, after hauling away mud-soaked carpets, I stood drenched, covering our computer and fridge with plastic while my mother and brother scrambled to line up pots, plates, and tubs to catch water pouring onto the bare floor. Standing there, I finally knew what it meant to be left without a roof: forgetting my sister had stripped her house bare under the rain.

Against all this, writing became my refuge. Losing my sister at eight, and years later living on that distant land without stable electricity or running water, left me feeling powerless; a child’s dreams could not buy a concrete roof or stop death. Yet in my notebook, under dim light, I made the rules. I breathed my sister’s soul into “Sarah,” wove our star-and-moon tale into her dialogue, and made my protagonist, “Adam,” keep the promise I could not: building her a “glass house.” Sunlight poured through every wall, banishing the cold rain that soaked my reality and barring any sickness from ever stealing someone we loved.

Building that world, however, forced me to confront my own weakness. In my story, I invented a drug that erases sad memories and numbs pain. While writing, a terrifying paradox hit home: erasing the pain of losing my sister inside my glass house meant erasing my love for her too, because grief is not the opposite of love, but its price, and the only proof left that we truly loved.

That truth stayed mere ink until I moved into my high school dorms. There, I traded my fragile glass house for a brotherhood of flesh and blood where “I” melted into “we” in every meal we split and every late night we spent pulling each other through homesickness. One night, a glass cup shattered as my roommate washed it, driving a shard deep into his palm. The blood would not stop, and my helplessness hurt more than the wound itself. I learned we don’t survive a harsh world by deleting pain, but by sharing it until it shrinks. From that bond, I wrote my novel’s most honest scene: eight starving children in a cell dividing seven small loaves into fifty-six equal pieces, so no one goes hungry alone.

This belief soon stepped off the page into our school library, which I was trusted to manage for all three years. One afternoon, a freshman sat across from my desk, his voice shaking as he considered quitting a writing contest, terrified his story about his late father would be judged. Closing the borrowing log, I pulled out my prize-winning novel summary and showed him the crossed-out margins where I once hid my own grief before learning to write honestly. We stayed until lockup, reworking his opening paragraph until his hands stopped trembling. The next morning, he quietly placed his finished entry on my desk.

I know my own day will come, and like my little star, I will leave only my name behind. I no longer write just to keep the rain off my sister’s memory. Now, I also sit beside others as they shelter their loved ones in words, so that when I close my eyes, my own little house will not be left roofless in the open.
